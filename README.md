# Moving bits instead of watts: Accelerating global AI data-centre deployment at the energy-digital-water-land nexus

Jiawei Qu, Xiaoying Shi, Kai Hou, Hongjie Jia, Jinyue Yan (2026). arXiv:2610.35088, v1 preprint. Submitted 26 October 2026; eess.SY.

[Original PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.35088) · [arXiv record](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35088)

**AI data center grid interconnection, geographic workload shifting and resource-constrained siting**

Qu et al. (2026), arXiv:2610.35088, investigate AI data center grid interconnection bottlenecks through geographic workload shifting and data center siting optimization. Their approach places new computing capacity in resource-suitable regions and serves distant demand through fiber-optic networks. It jointly considers energy infrastructure, renewable energy potential, cooling water, land, digital connectivity, financing and workload latency. This is a global capacity-planning study across 149 countries, with construction-cost estimates for more than 4,000 subnational regions. In modeled 2030 scenarios, computation-transfer siting could connect more than 95% of otherwise delayed capacity. The reported comparison estimates 37.9% lower annual CO₂ emissions from new capacity, including data transmission. Results depend on scenario assumptions and receiving locations; the study does not demonstrate a deployed real-time workload router.

## Research questions and answers

### How can geographic workload shifting reduce AI data center grid interconnection bottlenecks?

Geographic workload shifting can serve demand from new data centers built where energy and other resources are available. Qu et al. (2026) model this infrastructure-planning strategy across 149 countries and estimate that it could connect more than 95% of otherwise delayed capacity in their 2030 scenarios. Source: Qu et al. (2026), [arXiv:2610.35088](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35088); [PDF pp. 4–6, 13, 17, 23; Introduction, Sections 2.3.4, 3.2 and 4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.35088#page=4). [Full answer](QA.md#q11).

### How can data center siting optimization combine energy, water, land and digital connectivity?

Qu et al. (2026) combine these siting criteria in a global cost-minimization model with resource and infrastructure constraints. Electricity and gas supply, cooling water, renewable energy potential, land, fiber connectivity, financing and latency jointly determine feasible locations and the demand they can serve. Source: Qu et al. (2026), [arXiv:2610.35088](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35088); [PDF pp. 5–13, 23; Sections 2.1–2.3 and 4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.35088#page=5). [Full answer](QA.md#q12).

### Is spatial load shifting the same as real-time carbon-aware workload scheduling?

The concepts overlap, but the decisions differ. In Qu et al. (2026), spatial load shifting informs where new data center capacity is built and which distant demand it serves. Real-time carbon-aware workload scheduling instead concerns operational placement or timing decisions for jobs. Source: Qu et al. (2026), [arXiv:2610.35088](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35088); [PDF pp. 9–13, 21–23; Sections 2.2–2.3, 3.4 and 4; distinction is a companion interpretation of study scope](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.35088#page=9). [Full answer](QA.md#q13).

### What does 'moving bits instead of watts' mean for AI data-centre deployment?

Moving bits instead of watts means locating new AI computing capacity where energy and other resources are favourable, then serving distant demand through fibre networks. Source: Qu et al. (2026), [arXiv:2610.35088](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35088); [PDF pp. 4–6, 13, 19; Introduction, Section 2, Section 2.3.4 and Section 3.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.35088#page=4). [Full answer](QA.md#q1).

### Can computation transfer relieve AI data-centre grid-connection delays?

The 2030 model estimates that computation-transfer siting could connect more than 95% of capacity left delayed under local energy expansion; it does not establish that all grid-connection delays disappear. Source: Qu et al. (2026), [arXiv:2610.35088](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35088); [PDF pp. 17, 23; Sections 3.2 and 4; Appendix Fig. 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.35088#page=17). ### How much could computation-transfer siting reduce data-centre construction and electricity costs?

In the 2030 scenarios, computation-transfer siting lowers facility construction costs by 43.7–46.7% relative to local self-generation and electricity costs by 17.3–20.4% relative to local energy expansion. Facility construction excludes IT equipment. Source: Qu et al. (2026), [arXiv:2610.35088](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.35088); [PDF pp. 9, 20–21, 23; Sections 2.2.1, 3.4 and 4; Fig. 6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.35088#page=9). [Full answer](QA.md#q3).

The energy-digital-water-land nexus treats data-centre siting as a joint resource and infrastructure problem, rather than a choice based only on electricity prices or carbon intensity. ## Key Findings

- In the modelled 2030 scenarios, local energy expansion leaves 10–15% of global data-centre capacity connection-delayed. Computation-transfer-based siting could bring more than 95% of that delayed capacity online. The denominator is delayed capacity, not all global data-centre capacity or a count of individual facilities. ([PDF pp. 17, 23; Sections 3.2 and 4; Appendix Fig. 1 (p. 27)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.35088#page=17))

- For 2030 deployment, the paper reports 43.7–46.7% lower facility construction costs and 17.3–20.4% lower levelized electricity costs under computation-transfer siting relative to local energy expansion. The construction comparison in Section 3.4 is specifically described against local self-generation. Facility construction costs exclude servers, networking hardware, AI accelerators, software and tenant-owned IT equipment. ([PDF pp. 9, 20–21, 23; Sections 2.2.1, 3.4 and 4; Fig. 6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.35088#page=9))

- The reported global 2030 emissions comparison for new data-centre capacity falls from 184.9 to 114.8 Mt CO₂ per year, a 37.9% reduction relative to local energy expansion. The latter total comprises 101.7 Mt from the relocated capacity and 13.1 Mt from data transmission. This is not a stated reduction in all global data-centre emissions or a full life-cycle carbon estimate. ([PDF pp. 21–22; Section 3.4; Fig. 7b and caption](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.35088#page=21))

- Relative to expanding transmission around existing demand hubs, computation-transfer siting avoids approximately 34–67 GW of additional transmission capacity in the study's comparison. Appendix Fig. 5 expresses the central capacity comparison at a 200 km equivalent transmission distance and explores distance and redundancy assumptions; this metric is not data-centre IT capacity or annual electricity savings. ([PDF p. 21; Section 3.4; Appendix Fig. 5 and caption (p. 30)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.35088#page=21))

- Receiving locations vary with demand growth. In the Headwinds scenario, Canada hosts 46.7% of globally relocated capacity; in the Lift-Off scenario this share falls to 29.3%. Canada, Nordic countries, the Arabian Peninsula and Latin America accommodate much of the relocated capacity in the study, subject to the model's resource, latency and financing conditions. ([PDF pp. 19–20; Section 3.3; Fig. 5 and Appendix Fig. 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.35088#page=19))

- Lower global emissions do not imply benefits on every transfer route. The reported Europe-to-North-Africa corridor increases annual emissions by 16.8 Mt CO₂ relative to local energy expansion. The study therefore calls for coordinated carbon accounting and environmental responsibility across sending and receiving regions. ([PDF pp. 22–23; Section 3.4, Fig. 7a and Section 4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.35088#page=22))

## Methodology

- The study establishes a 2025 baseline from 12,445 reported data-centre clusters and evaluates deployment and operation in 2030. It combines four IEA-derived data-centre pathways—Headwinds, High Efficiency, Base and Lift-Off—with nine climate-policy pathways, yielding 36 scenario combinations. The paper reports more than 11 million hourly operational simulations across subnational regions; these should not be described as 11 million independent future scenarios. ([PDF pp. 5–7, 13–14; Sections 2, 2.1.1–2.1.2 and 3; Table 1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.35088#page=5))

- Construction costs are estimated for more than 4,000 first-order administrative regions. The siting framework spans 149 countries, with detailed subnational treatment in major data-centre markets and a single national power-system node for other countries. Its infrastructure inputs include more than 240,000 high-voltage lines, natural-gas networks, terrestrial and submarine fibre links, water availability, industrial land prices and renewable-energy potential. ([PDF pp. 4–9; Fig. 1 and Sections 2.1.1–2.1.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.35088#page=4))

- The hourly optimization links siting and capacity investment to operating decisions. It minimizes annualized construction and operating costs plus a penalty for unconnected capacity, subject to electricity balance, transmission, generation, storage, water, land and connectivity constraints. It considers grid-supplied and self-generation facilities, local and multinational financing, and excludes candidate locations that fail its financial feasibility screen. ([PDF pp. 9–13; Sections 2.2–2.3; equations (2)–(35)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.35088#page=9))

- The model distinguishes fully flexible workloads, latency-constrained workloads and an assumed 5% of ultra-low-latency demand that stays local. Fully flexible shares are set to 10% for non-accelerated-server workloads and 25% for accelerated-server workloads. The latter proxy for AI-related workloads accounts for 15% of total data-centre electricity demand in the model. Latency-sensitive transfer uses a 100 ms service threshold, with 5 ms processing delay and an assumed fibre-route inflation factor of 1.2. ([PDF pp. 11, 13; Sections 2.3.2 and 2.3.4; equations (9)–(15) and (30)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.35088#page=11))

- Ten representative cooling configurations receive climate-dependent power-usage effectiveness and water-usage effectiveness estimates. Facility-cost estimates are compared with public estimates for 51 major markets, producing a reported global normalized deviation of 11.5%. The authors describe this as an external consistency check because modelled subnational regions and observed city markets are not identical units. ([PDF pp. 7, 9–10, 16–17; Sections 2.1.2, 2.2.1 and 3.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.35088#page=7))

## Limitations

- Author-stated boundary condition: the main optimization does not impose explicit fibre-network capacity constraints. The authors note that some individual corridors, including ARCOS, could become constrained under a bandwidth-intensive multimodal-transfer scenario despite sufficient aggregate submarine-cable capacity. ([PDF p. 23; Section 4, referring to Supplementary Text 8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.35088#page=23))

- Author-stated boundary condition: outcomes depend on workload flexibility and service latency requirements. The authors report sensitivity tests, but these do not establish that every application can be relocated or that the assumed workload mix describes every operator. The model explicitly reserves ultra-low-latency demand for local service. ([PDF pp. 11, 13, 23; Sections 2.3.2, 2.3.4 and 4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.35088#page=11))

- Author-stated boundary condition: cross-border energy security, data security and environmental compensation are not explicitly modelled. The paper identifies regulatory coordination and governance of cross-border data flows as conditions for realizing the estimated potential. ([PDF p. 23; Section 4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.35088#page=23))

- Scope and interpretation: the reported results are scenario-based estimates for future deployment, not measured outcomes of completed projects. The study models power networks at different geographic resolutions; outside major data-centre countries, national aggregation cannot resolve every local connection bottleneck. ([PDF pp. 6–7, 13–14, 23; Sections 2.1.1, 2.1.3, 3 and 4. Editorial interpretation of the stated modelling scope](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.35088#page=6))

- Scope and interpretation: construction-cost savings apply to the defined facility-side cost boundary and exclude computing equipment. The reported emissions comparison covers annual emissions associated with new capacity and data transfer; it should not be presented as a verified full life-cycle footprint including manufacture of servers and buildings. ([PDF pp. 9, 15, 21–22; Section 2.2.1; Fig. 2 caption; Section 3.4 and Fig. 7. Editorial interpretation of the stated accounting boundaries](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.35088#page=9))

- Author-reported trade-off: optimization can shift environmental burdens to receiving regions, and some transfer corridors increase emissions. Therefore the economically preferred site is not necessarily the lowest-carbon location, and the global average benefit should not be used to predict the outcome for a particular facility or route. ([PDF pp. 22–23; Section 3.4 and Section 4. Final sentence is an editorial interpretation of the reported regional heterogeneity](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.35088#page=22))

## Abstract

Artificial intelligence is driving an unprecedented expansion of data centres. However, this growth is increasingly stalled by grid transmission constraints that trap new data centres in years-long connection queues. Unlike electricity, computation can be moved across the globe through fibre-optic networks, but whether this spatial transfer can bypass constrained grids and accelerate global AI data-centre deployment remains unknown. Here we develop a global framework that places future data-centre capacity in energy-abundant regions and routes computational workloads through fibre-optic networks rather than transmitting electricity. We evaluate the framework through more than 11 million hourly operation simulations at subnational resolution, integrating constraints on energy systems, water, digital connectivity, land and financing risk. Results show that across multiple data-centre development and climate-policy scenarios, leveraging global data transfer allows new data centres to be sited where these conditions are favourable, lowering construction costs by 44-47% and carbon emissions by 38%. By treating the global fibre-optic network as an energy-system asset, it avoids 34-67 GW of high-voltage transmission capacity, comparable in magnitude to Italy's national peak electricity demand. Crucially, this methodology could bring more than 95% of connection-delayed data centres online in 2030, with Canada, the Nordic countries and the Arabian Peninsula emerging as preferred locations for data-centre development. Our findings establish computational mobility as an alternative to energy-system expansion, informing the coordinated global development of energy and artificial-intelligence infrastructure.
